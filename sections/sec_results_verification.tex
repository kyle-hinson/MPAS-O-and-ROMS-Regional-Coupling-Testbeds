The results section is separated in three parts. We first present rigorous numerical experiments that verify that the designed volumetric remapping algorithm is accurate and consistent. Then we analyze the computational performance of the remapper implementation, which exploits the tensor product structure by utilizing OpenMP thread-level parallelism. Finally, we will present realistic one-way coupled \MPAS-\ROMS simulation results that demonstrate the validity of the approach, and emphasize the fine-scale feature resolution in both open-ocean and coastal domains that are not captured by coarse global \MPAS ocean model.

\subsection{Verification of transfer}\label{conv:sec:results-verification}

We present the remapping algorithm verfiication study in two parts. First, through the one-dimensional axial refinement study, and next the full volumetric remap convergence study that quantifies optimal downscaling resolutions for improving feature resolution.

\subsubsection{Test configuration}\label{conv:sec:verification-method}
The accuracy of the composed projection is assessed with a round trip. A source field $\Phi$ is transferred forward as $\Psi=\mathcal{V}_{M\to R}(W_{M\to R}\Phi)$ and then returned as $\tilde{\Phi}=\mathcal{V}_{R\to M}(W_{R\to M}\Psi)$. We measure the round-trip error $e_{jk}=\tilde{\Phi}_{jk}-\Phi_{jk}$, which lives on the source mesh, using volume-weighted $L_1$ and $L_2$ norms and an unweighted $L_\infty$ norm:
\begin{align}
  \Lone &= \frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I},k} A^M_j\,\Delta \zmpas_{jk}\,|e_{jk}|}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I},k} A^M_j\,\Delta \zmpas_{jk}},\qquad
  \Ltwo = \left(\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I},k} A^M_j\,\Delta \zmpas_{jk}\,e_{jk}^2}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I},k} A^M_j\,\Delta \zmpas_{jk}}\right)^{1/2},\\
  \Linf &= \max_{j\in\mathcal{I},k}|e_{jk}|,
  \label{conv:eq:norms}
\end{align}
where $\Delta \zmpas_{jk}$ is the thickness of level $k$ in source column $j$, so that $A^M_j\,\Delta \zmpas_{jk}$ is the cell volume, and $\mathcal{I}$ denotes the set of source cells with complete horizontal coverage in the sense of the classification below. Partial cells, and columns without a valid vertical reconstruction, are excluded, so the three-dimensional support on which the norms are evaluated varies somewhat with target refinement. The round trip characterizes the composed operator and does not bound the one-way forcing error, which is the quantity of interest when the transfer is used to drive a regional simulation.

The smooth field used throughout the convergence study is the tensor-product sinusoid
\begin{equation}
 R(\lambda,\varphi,z)=\sin\!\left(\frac{m_\lambda\pi(\lambda-\lambda_0)}{L_\lambda}\right)
 \sin\!\left(\frac{m_\varphi\pi(\varphi-\varphi_0)}{L_\varphi}\right)
 \sin\!\left(\frac{m_z\pi|z|}{H_0}\right),
 \label{conv:eq:analytic}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ and $\varphi$ are longitude and latitude, $(\lambda_0,\varphi_0)$ is the southwest corner of the target bounding box, $L_\lambda$ and $L_\varphi$ are its angular extents, and $(m_\lambda,m_\varphi,m_z)$ are the mode numbers in the two horizontal directions and the vertical. The field has unit amplitude, $(m_\lambda,m_\varphi,m_z)=(2,2,1)$, and $H_0=\SI{4000}{\metre}$. It is transferred alongside salinity, temperature, and the two velocity components from one daily-mean source state.

Writing $A_{\mathrm{bbox}}$ for the area of the common bounding box, $n_{\mathrm{bbox}}$ for the number of source cells whose centres fall inside it, and $n_{\mathrm{cells}}$ for the number of target cells, the representative spacings are $\hM=\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{bbox}}/n_{\mathrm{bbox}}}$ and $\hR=\sqrt{A_{\mathrm{bbox}}/n_{\mathrm{cells}}}$, and their ratio is $\hRatio=\hR/\hM$. Four nested target grids, r1 (coarsest) through r4 (finest), are used in each region. Their inventory is given in Table~\ref{conv:tab:grid-inventory}.

\paragraph{Coverage classification.}\label{sec:classification}
Because the \ROMS mesh is a bounded region embedded in a global \MPAS source mesh, every target \ROMS cell is labeled based on whether it is within the convex hull of the \MPAS coverage mesh \cite{mahadevan2020coupling}. Let $f^M_j$ and $f^R_i$ denote the covered fractions of source cell $j$ and target cell $i$, obtained from the overlap areas $A_{ij}$ of the FV supermesh. Cells with $f=1$ are \emph{interior}, and those with $0<f<1$ are only \emph{partial} covered. The interior set, written as $\mathcal{I}$, is the support on which the error norms of \S\ref{conv:sec:verification-method} are evaluated. Coverage-weighted conservation is a separate map property and condition:
\begin{equation}
	\sum_i A^R_i f^R_i\,\Xi_{i,k}
	=\sum_j A^M_j f^M_j\,\Phi_{j,k}.
	\label{conv:eq:bilin-conservation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{conv:eq:bilin-conservation} is weighted by the covered fractions on both sides, so it constrains only the shared support of the two meshes. It is satisfied by the conservative FV map of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight} but not by the normalized bilinear map of Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:bilin-weight}, which is the trade discussed in \S\ref{sec:why-not-fv}.

\subsubsection{One-dimensional axial refinement study}\label{conv:sec:axial-refinement}

Figure~\ref{conv:fig:physical-1d-workflow} isolates the axial factor under joint source and target level refinements. It is a reduced representation of the geometry in Fig.~\ref{conv:fig:coord-mismatch} where the selected nonuniform \MPAS columns retain the physical $z^*$ layer-thickness patterns of the Gulf Stream and Bay of Bengal source domains, while the target \ROMS interfaces are regenerated from the same terrain-following stretching families used by the regional grids. The matching and fixed-bottom extrapolation cases respectively represent a target column embedded in the resolved \MPAS column [SSH, Bathymetry] range, and a \ROMS column extending slightly beyond it due to the stair stepping nature of the bathymetry at higher resolution. Thus the one-dimensional sequence retains the vertical-coordinate mismatch, and refinement mechanism of the regional cases while removing influence of horizontal remap effects. For a smooth sinusoidal profile and the tested cases, the conservative axial projection displays the expected first-order convergence over. The PCHIP point projection and the conservative axial projection have distinct output semantics: the former is a non-conservative point-value projection of average values, whereas the latter is evaluated from target-layer integral residuals. The figure therefore compares the two workflow contracts without treating their discrete $L_2$ measures as interchangeable.

For the separable transfer in Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:tensor}, the combined error may be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
  E_{3D}\lesssim C_h h_h^{p_h}+C_z h_z^{p_z}.
  \label{conv:eq:tensor-order}
\end{equation}
When horizontal and axial resolutions are jointly refined at comparable rates, the lower directional observed order limits the asymptotic rate of the tensor-product workflow. Thus the observed axial first-order behavior supplies the relevant $\mathcal{O}(h)$ reference for the tested joint refinement. The one-dimensional experiment measures the axial factor only; it does not independently establish a complete three-dimensional rate or a universal order for every field, mesh, or limiter state.

\subsubsection{Convergence and quantification of error sources}\label{conv:sec:conv}

In general, a proper convergence study requires that both the source \MPAS mesh and target \ROMS mesh are refined uniformly. However, in the current study, we consider that the given \MPAS global mesh resolution is fixed, and generate a series of embedded, refined \ROMS meshes to perform error analysis.

Figures~\ref{conv:fig:gs-convergence} and~\ref{conv:fig:bob-convergence} show the interior round-trip norms of Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:norms} versus $\hRatio$ for the two regions. Scalar errors are normalized by representative absolute field magnitudes.

Errors decrease over the coarse portion of both sequences, but the real-field errors do not show consistent changes beyond r3 i.e., error plateaus. The error convergence sequences suggest that $\hRatio\approx0.2$, roughly a fivefold refinement in \ROMS resolution, is a useful starting point when choosing a regional grid, and targets at or below $\hRatio\approx0.1$ should be justified by a refinement study for domain of interest when balancing accuracy and computational efficiency. Given that the heuristic has been tested for both the open-ocean and coastal regional domain, this can be generally applied as a good starting point when downscaling global solutions to regional domains.  

More generally, for a given parent mesh a short convergence sequence of this kind is the practical way to identify the resolution beyond which further refinement of the regional grid ceases to be worthwhile. As in any multiscale configuration, the lateral boundary conditions supplied from \MPAS carry coarse scale errors that cannot be resolved by \ROMS, or by the forcing itself, as these do not diminish as the target mesh is refined. In other words, relative to $\hRatio$ the errors remain $\mathcal{O}(1)$. This is especially important to note when we are interested in performing two-way coupled multiscale transfers as the \MPAS to \ROMS solution transfer dominates error propagation.  In such cases, pushing $\hRatio << 0.1$ yields no perceptible gain in fidelity. The cost, however, continues to grow: the number of target cells scales as $\hRatio^{-2}$ and a CFL-limited time step shrinks in proportion to the spacing, so integrating a fixed period becomes roughly $\hRatio^{-3}$ more expensive. Choosing the target resolution near the point where the sequence flattens therefore captures nearly all of the accessible accuracy at optimal computational cost.

The order of the composed scheme follows from its vertical component. Imposing conservation on the vertical interpolant, in the sense that each target-layer integral of the reconstructed profile is reproduced exactly, constrains the reconstruction to reproduce the layer mean rather than the pointwise profile, and the resulting operator is first order in the layer thickness. Since the tensor product in Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:tensor} is no more accurate than its least accurate factor, the composed three-dimensional transfer is first order overall, irrespective of the formal accuracy of the horizontal map. The measured decrease over the convergent portion of both sequences is consistent with this expectation, although four grids are too few to fit an asymptotic rate. Relaxing the constraint admits alternatives: a quasi-interpolant that reproduces the profile rather than its layer integrals can achieve higher order on smooth fields, but forfeits exact conservation, monotonicity, or both, depending on how it is constructed. Which trade is appropriate depends on the application, since a scheme that attains higher order by violating monotonicity may introduce spurious extrema in sharp features such as the thermocline, and one that sacrifices conservation is poorly suited to long integrations where tracer inventories must be maintained. The Bay of Bengal r3--r4 comparison deserves particular caution, because the number of columns excluded as below-depth rises from 18,309 to 166,770 between the two grids. The norms are then evaluated on different supports, even though the real-field $L_1$ and $L_2$ values differ by only a few percent.

Because the round trip chains the forward and reverse horizontal maps with the forward and reverse vertical remaps, it cannot by itself indicate which stage limits the result. Controlled experiments on the analytic field, holding the \MPAS source fixed while refining only the \ROMS target, bound the individual contributions. A constant field carried forward and back through the horizontal map alone is reproduced to roundoff, as required by the normalization in Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:bilin-weight}. In the tensor product split, the composed one-way error, measured against the analytic solution sampled on the target grid, then varies by about 4\% in the Gulf Stream and 5\% in the Bay of Bengal across the whole r1--r4 sequence, which is what we expect when the fixed parent representation, rather than the target spacing, sets the achievable accuracy. The round trip over the same sequence falls by roughly a factor of 23 and 30 respectively, since errors incurred on the forward leg can be undone on the reverse leg; the round trip is consequently not the one-way error plus a correction, and the two should not be compared directly. These statements apply to the controlled analytic configuration and do not settle the real-field r3--r4 common-support caveat.

Errors grow with depth, and the salinity floors in the two regions differ by about two orders of magnitude, which is consistent with their different source scales and coastal gradients. The persistent $L_\infty$ salinity errors point to under-resolved near-surface stratification and to coastal localization that shifts with refinement, though it is hard to diagnose these exact contributions towards the overall roundtrip error accumulation quantitatively.

\subsection{Performance}\label{conv:sec:performance}
The benchmark projects four fields from a source mesh of $5.7\times10^6$ polyhedra to a target mesh of $8\times10^6$ elements on one Perlmutter CPU node, using 1--64 OpenMP threads and $N_z=50$ and 100.

\subsection{Bathymetry treatment and regional applications}\label{conv:sec:bathysmooth}
The projected bathymetry can generate sigma-coordinate pressure-gradient errors over steep slopes. Writing $H_i$ for the depth of target cell $i$, $q_i=\log H_i$ for its logarithm, $\mathcal{A}(i)$ for the set of edge-neighbours of that cell, and $m$ for the iteration counter, we smooth the logarithm by one-ring averaging,
\begin{equation}
 q_i^{(m+1)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}(i)|}\sum_{i'\in\mathcal{A}(i)}q_{i'}^{(m)},\qquad H_i^{(m+1)}=\exp q_i^{(m+1)},
 \label{conv:eq:shapiro}
\end{equation}
with clipping to the source range. Smoothing by Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:shapiro} stops once the steepest remaining neighbour contrast satisfies
\begin{equation}
 r_{\max}^{(m)}=\max_i\max_{i'\in\mathcal{A}(i)}\frac{q_i^{(m)}-q_{i'}^{(m)}}{q_i^{(m)}+q_{i'}^{(m)}}<0.08,
 \label{conv:eq:slope-stop}
\end{equation}
or after 15 iterations, whichever comes first. The resulting bathymetry is shown in Fig.~\ref{conv:fig:smoothing}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/conv_bathy_smoothing.png}
\caption{Projected Bay of Bengal bathymetry before and after logarithmic neighbor averaging.}
\label{conv:fig:smoothing}
\end{figure}

Smoothing the logarithm of the bathymetry changes the round-trip errors by less than 5\% and was sufficient to stabilize the Bay of Bengal integration. That domain also required vertical smoothing of the remapped scalar profiles, which removed a warm anomaly between 100 and 200 m near masked coastlines. The two treatments act on different quantities and we report their effects separately. Elsewhere the regional applications use the projected parent geometry, except where coastal or estuarine topography forces us to blend it with the native target bathymetry and to retain the target land mask. Chesapeake Bay is the clearest example: the parent mesh cannot represent the estuarine geometry at all, so the boundary and initial fields there must be prescribed or blended, and the blending weights we used are not specified precisely enough for the case to be reproduced.
