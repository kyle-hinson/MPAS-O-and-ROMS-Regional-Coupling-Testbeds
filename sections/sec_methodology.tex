
The global \MPAS model uses Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT) to generate a variable-resolution polygonal mesh, which provides a smooth transition between coastal and deep-ocean regions and avoids the abrupt refinement interfaces that degrade accuracy in long integrations. The regional \ROMS model, at typically higher spatiotemporal resolution, uses a boundary-fitted curvilinear grid, so that a regionally structured mesh is embedded within the unstructured global polygonal mesh. We will discuss the problem setup, the complications that arise due to variations in axial coordinate systems~\citep{petersen2015evaluation}, and describe the algorithm that achieves consistent embeddings to transfer the forcing conditions accurately and efficiently.

\subsection{Problem statement and notation}\label{sec:problem}

The central design constraint is that the \MPAS and \ROMS
discretizations differ in \emph{both} the horizontal tessellation
(unstructured Voronoi vs.\ structured curvilinear) \emph{and} the
vertical coordinate (geopotential $z^*$ vs.\ terrain-following
$\sigma$) systems, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{conv:fig:coord-mismatch}.


Let the {\MPAS} global ocean mesh cover a region of interest with
$n_M$ Voronoi cells, each carrying a column of tracer values on
$K_M$ geopotential $z^*$ levels $\{\zmpas_k\}_{k=1}^{K_M}$, whose
thicknesses respond to the free surface but which are indexed
identically in every column. Let the \ROMS{}
grid have $n_R$ horizontal cells, each with a column of
$K_R$ terrain-following (sigma) levels whose absolute depths
$\{z_\ell^{(i)}\}_{\ell=1}^{K_R}$ depend on the local bathymetry
$H_i$ and free-surface elevation $\zeta_i$ through the Song--Haidvogel
stretching transformation~\citep{song1994semi,shchepetkin2005regional}.
A three-dimensional \MPAS field is then given by the array
$\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{n_M \times K_M}$ and the projected \ROMS 
regional field is $\Psi \in \mathbb{R}^{n_R \times K_R}$. The problem at hand is to compute a consistent and conservative three-dimensional solution transfer of $\Psi$ from global \MPAS models containing $\Phi$ to achieve a  multiscale solution framework with submesoscale resolution and consistent forcing conditions.

Note that a fully
three-dimensional intersection to compute a supermesh~\citep{ullrich2015arbitrary} of two such cell complexes can be computed, but in general will involve $O(n_M K_M n_R K_R)$ computational complexity to decipher candidate overlaps (worst case),
and can produce multiple sliver polyhedra whose quadratures may be ill-conditioned.
We instead exploit the fact that the \MPAS{} $\zmpas$ coordinate shares a
common level indexing across all \MPAS columns: level $k$ refers to
the same layer of the discretization everywhere, even though its
physical thickness varies from column to column with the free surface, the bottom topography and axial masking. A single horizontal map can therefore be
applied slab by slab at fixed $k$, which factors the volumetric transfer into a
horizontal remap and a one-dimensional vertical remap. The physical
depths that the vertical remap requires are recovered per column
afterwards, and so the factorization does not assume that the levels are
horizontally flat. Instead, we assume that interpolating within a
level index is meaningful, which holds when the physical $\zmpas$ surfaces vary
slowly relative to the horizontal stencil.

Throughout the manuscript, the \MPAS model is referred to as the \emph{source} and \ROMS as the \emph{target}. However, note that the algorithmic design is agnostic to the structured or unstructured nature of the underlying topology. In terms of notation, we also use the subscript $j$ for \MPAS cell indices, and $i$ for \ROMS cells, while $k$ and $\ell$ index \MPAS and \ROMS vertical levels respectively. Uppercase $\Phi,\Xi,\Psi$ denote whole
three-dimensional arrays, and the corresponding lowercase $\phi,\psi$
denote a single horizontal slab extracted at a fixed level, so that
$\phi_j=\Phi_{j,k}$ for the level $k$ under discussion. Cell areas carry the mesh as a superscript: $A^M_j$ is the
area of \MPAS cell $j$, $A^R_i$ that of \ROMS cell $i$, and $A_{ij}$
the area of their intersection. All geometry is treated on the unit
sphere, and spherical areas on the manifold are computed by \emph{L'Huilier}'s excess
formula on the unit sphere, scaled appropriately by the \MPAS Earth radius.

\subsection{Separable volumetric remapping}\label{conv:sec:methods}

Writing $\Xi$ for the intermediate field that results from transferring
every \MPAS level horizontally, and $\mathcal{V}$ for the column-wise
vertical remap, the composed transfer operation is
\begin{equation}
  \Xi_{i,k}=\sum_j W_{ij}\Phi_{j,k},\qquad \Psi=\mathcal{V}(\Xi),
  \label{conv:eq:tensor}
\end{equation}

where the horizontal map $W$ is reused unchanged at every \MPAS level
and $\mathcal{V}$ acts independently on each \ROMS column. This
prism-based 2D$\times$1D construction avoids sliver polyhedra while
retaining the column geometry that the vertical remap requires. The two
factors are in turn developed in \S \ref{sec:horizontal} and
\S\ref{sec:vertical}. A schematic of the volumetric remapping algorithm is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:remap_methods}.

\subsection{Horizontal remap as a sparse linear map}\label{sec:horizontal}

Several choices are available for computing projections or interpolants from a global spherical manifold mesh like \MPAS onto an embedded \ROMS regional domain. The standard approach is the use of $L_2$ minimization~\citep{dukowicz1987accurate} that preserves both global and local integrals and is therefore exactly conservative. The low order variant of the minimizer is also truly property-preserving (monotone). %In the coarse-to-fine regime considered here it also imprints the \MPAS tessellation on the \ROMS field, which is why we do not use it for tracer transfer, as \S\ref{sec:why-not-fv} develops in detail and Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh-imprinting} demonstrates. We first set out both constructions.
Alternatively, one could also utilize variations of delaunay-based natural neighbor or bilinear interpolants, while preserving the integrals as designed in implementations like TempestRemap~\citep{ullrich2015arbitrary}. The implications of some of these choices will be further explored.

For a fixed \MPAS depth level $k$, the horizontal factor $W$ of
Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:tensor} acts on the level slab
$\phi=\Phi_{:,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_M}$ as the sparse linear map
\begin{equation}
	\psi_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n_M} W_{ij}\, \phi_j ,
	\qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{n_R \times n_M},
	\label{eq:linear-map}
\end{equation}
as defined by~\citet{ullrich2015arbitrary,ullrich2016tempestremap}. Two
specific constructions of the weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear-map} are
considered below, and they primarily differ in whether strict conservation or
smoothness is enforced for problem at hand.

\paragraph{Conservative finite-volume (FV) map.}
The first-order conservative finite-volume linear-map ~\citep{jones1999scrip} is computed based on the full supermesh in order to account for the contributions from all partial subcell geometry neighbors from the \MPAS mesh overlapping the \ROMS elements. This sets the
weight to the area of geometric overlap between \ROMS cell $i$ and
\MPAS cell $j$, normalized by the \ROMS area,
\begin{equation}
	W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij} \;=\; \frac{|\Omega_i \cap \Omega_j|}{|\Omega_i|}
	\;=\; \frac{A_{ij}}{A^R_i},
	\label{eq:fv-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_i$, $\Omega_j$ are the (spherical) \ROMS and \MPAS
polygons and $A_{ij}$ their intersection area. This map is
\emph{conservative only over the \ROMS bounded subdomain}, a
qualification that matters here because the \ROMS{} domain is a subset embedded in the global \MPAS{} mesh. 


%\paragraph{Coverage classification.}
% \label{sec:classification}
Note that the \ROMS mesh is a bounded region embedded in the global \MPAS mesh, and so every \ROMS cell is labeled based on whether it is within the convex hull of the \MPAS coverage mesh~\citep{mahadevan2020coupling}. Let $f^M_j$ and $f^R_i$ denote the covered fractions of \MPAS cell $j$ and \ROMS cell $i$, obtained from the overlap areas $A_{ij}$ of the FV supermesh. Cells with $f=1$ are \emph{interior}, and those with $0<f<1$ are only \emph{partially} covered. The interior set, written as $\mathcal{I}$, is the support on which the error norms of \S\ref{conv:sec:verification-method} are evaluated.

It is satisfied by the conservative FV map of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight} but not by the normalized bilinear map of Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:bilin-weight}, which is the trade discussed in \S\ref{sec:why-not-fv}.

Note that since
$\sum_i A_{ij} = A^M_j$ holds only for a \MPAS cell fully covered by the
union of \ROMS cells, the area-weighted integral identity
$\sum_i A^R_i \psi_i = \sum_j A^M_j \phi_j$ is exact for \MPAS cells
interior to the \ROMS boundary and needs to include a fractional weighting (computed from supermesh construction) for \MPAS cells that only partially overlaps with \ROMS domain. Hence computing the conservation of fluxes and states in the \ROMS domain boundary strictly requires topological intersections, without which we
can only enforce weak conservation over $\mathcal{I}$. 

The map is first-order accurate and, being
a convex average of the \MPAS values ($W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij}\ge 0$,
$\sum_j W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij}=1$ on fully-covered cells), it is
property-preserving on those cells: the transferred values are bounded
by the \MPAS value range in the covering mesh.

\paragraph{Normalized bilinear map.}
The bilinear map locates each \ROMS cell centroid within the
\MPAS triangulation, forms bilinear interpolation weights
$b_{ij}$ from the enclosing \MPAS elements, and row-normalizes them, following the linear-map framework of~\citet{ullrich2015arbitrary,ullrich2016tempestremap}.
\begin{equation}
	W^{\mathrm{BL}}_{ij}
	\;=\; \frac{b_{ij}}{\sum_{j'} b_{ij'}} ,
	\label{conv:eq:bilin-weight}
\end{equation}
so that constant fields are reproduced exactly
($\sum_j W^{\mathrm{BL}}_{ij}=1$). The bilinear map is not globally
conservative by construction, since its column sums are not area ratios as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight}. It is, however, formally second-order accurate on smooth fields \citep{ullrich2015arbitrary} and, more importantly for the downscaling setup, it does not tie the \ROMS values to the piecewise-constant average of a single \MPAS cell. When the \ROMS centroid lies within the enclosing \MPAS element the weights are nonnegative and form a convex combination, so the map is property-preserving: the transferred values are bounded by the \MPAS value range in the enclosing element. Its second-order horizontal accuracy does not raise the order of the composed transfer, which is limited by the conservative vertical remap of \S\ref{sec:vertical}, as discussed in \S\ref{conv:sec:conv}.



\subsection{Issues with conservative FV map for tracers}\label{sec:why-not-fv}

The monotone, conservative finite-volume (FV) map in \eqref{eq:fv-weight} is the lowest-order case of the \(L_2\)-minimization framework used for general mesh remapping \citep{ullrich2015arbitrary,ullrich2016tempestremap}. Given compatible bases on the two meshes, the transferred field minimizes the error in the integral of the field defined on the overlapping domain between a source and target mesh topology
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega}(\phi-\psi)^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega ,
	\label{eq:l2-min}
\end{equation}
leading to a discrete system $\mathcal{W}\Phi=\Psi$, where $\mathcal{W}$ is the mass matrix over the \ROMS and \MPAS bases. Minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:l2-min} over the overlap domain is what makes the resulting map conservative, and at lowest order it recovers the area-ratio weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight}. Higher-order conservative, monotone, and smooth alternatives are available \citep{lauritzen2008monotone,lee1997mba}, but given the strong gradients in the 3D ocean states, we focus here only on the first-order FV map and the normalized bilinear map in \eqref{conv:eq:bilin-weight}, which capture the main trade-off relevant to \MPAS--\ROMS downscaling experiments.
%

This factor is not used for tracer soltion transfer in regional coupled runs however, to avoid mesh imprinting artifacts in the remapped fields.


%Under first-order FV remapping, all fine \ROMS cells lying within a given \MPAS Voronoi cell receive the same \MPAS cell average. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh-imprinting} demonstrates the effect of using the two types of projections namely the conservative FV(1) method and the bilinear algorithm for the bathymetry field over the refined North Atlantic domain. In the configurations considered here, the \ROMS cells are typically much smaller (5x on average) than the \MPAS ocean model cells, leading to $\hRatio\approx0.2$. The conservative FV-based map projection result (center) reproduces the \MPAS Voronoi tessellation as piecewise-constant patches with sharp artificial steps, while the normalized bilinear projection (right) remains smooth. 
%
These discontinuities are not physical gradients. They are introduced solely by the transfer operator and can inject grid-scale noise or adjustment features aligned with the \MPAS mesh when the field is used to initialize or force a regional model. The problem is especially consequential for fields that should vary smoothly at the regional scale. Bathymetry is the sharpest illustration because it sets the \ROMS sigma-coordinate geometry: an imprint in the transferred depth field perturbs the vertical grid itself, not only the horizontal representation, so the artifact propagates into every subsequent column operation.

The normalized bilinear map removes the leading-order imprint by interpolating continuously across the \MPAS mesh rather than assigning constant cell averages, at the cost of exact global conservation. For bathymetry, SSH and initial-condition forcing, we can regard this as an acceptable compromise to avoid imprinting effects, since \ROMS evolves its tracer components with its own conservative transport scheme, whereas mesh-imprinted forcing introduces artificial gradients that can persist in the interior solution. However, when the transferred solution must be conservative during the downscaling operation, the bilinear map can be followed by a rescaling operation (post-processing step) of the transferred field against the \MPAS fields defined on the shared support. This restores global integrals at the cost of losing exact monotonicity in high-gradient regions~\citep{mahadevan2022mira}.

Higher-order quasi-interpolants offer a third route. Multilevel B-spline approximation (MBA) builds a smooth field from a hierarchy of control lattices~\citep{lee1997mba}, while Generalized Moving Least-Squares and Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory reconstructions instead fit local stencils, the latter with nonlinear weights that suppress oscillations near discontinuities~\citep{mahadevan2022mira}. Each would need a normalization step to recover conservation, and limiters to recover monotonicity. We have not pursued these here.

\subsection{Vertical remap of each column}\label{sec:vertical}

Each intermediate column is assigned physical \MPAS depths taken from the nearest \MPAS column, and is then remapped onto the local \ROMS depths, where the terrain-following depths follow from the \ROMS bathymetry and free-surface ocean elevation (SSH). The three-dimensional transfer uses the conservative axial projection: the property preserved in the vertical direction is the integral of the reconstructed profile over each \ROMS layer, which for the piecewise-average degrees of freedom originating from finite-volume solvers amounts to a remap of \MPAS layer averages. A monotone piecewise cubic-Hermite interpolant (PCHIP) is also available as a point-value projection, and the two are compared in \S\ref{conv:sec:axial-refinement}, and because it reproduces the profile rather than its layer integrals, it reaches higher order on smooth data but does not conserve, which is why the conservative projection is the one used for the transfers reported here.

Two situations require the column to be extended beyond the range \MPAS actually resolves. Supplying a full level slab requires values below the deepest wet \MPAS level, where \MPAS masks its axial layers, and these are held at the deepest wet value, equivalent to nearest-neighbor extrapolation, and the extended tail is discarded once the column is restricted to its physical depth range. Where the \ROMS column extends beyond the \MPAS range, the profile is likewise held at the nearest endpoint rather than extrapolated. Both rules apply to tracers, while velocities are instead set to zero outside the \MPAS range, imposing a no-flow condition. These choices matter most where bathymetry varies sharply, and we return to their consequences in \S\ref{conv:sec:bathysmooth}.


%We should emphasize that these are properties of the two component operators. Neither individually, nor in combination, do they guarantee that the composed three-dimensional transfer conserves volume-integrated quantities or preserves bounds.

It is noted that the zonal and meridional velocities are transferred component-wise as scalar, Earth-relative fields at horizontal cell centers. No rotation to the curvilinear basis, interpolation to polygonal edges, or discrete divergence constraints are imposed in this transfer. The velocity diagnostics presented in this manuscript measure componentwise transport in a fixed basis. Use of consistent mimetic schemes to dynamically transfer vector fields remains outside the present scope of the paper and will be pursued in future work.
