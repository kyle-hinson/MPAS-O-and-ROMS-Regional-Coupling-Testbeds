

The global \MPAS model uses Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT) to generate a variable-resolution polygonal mesh, which provides a smooth transition between coastal and deep-ocean regions and avoids the abrupt refinement interfaces that degrade accuracy in long term integrations. The regional \ROMS model, at typically higher spatiotemporal resolution, uses a boundary-fitted curvilinear grid, so that a regionally structured mesh is embedded within the unstructured global polygonal mesh. We will discuss the problem setup, the complications that arise due to variations in axial coordinate systems~\citep{petersen2015evaluation}, and describe the algorithm that achieves consistent embeddings to transfer the forcing conditions accurately and efficiently.

\subsection{Problem statement and notation}\label{sec:problem}

The central design constraint is that the \MPAS and \ROMS
discretizations differ in \emph{both} the horizontal tessellation
(unstructured Voronoi vs.\ structured curvilinear) \emph{and} the
vertical coordinate (geopotential $z^*$ vs.\ terrain-following
$\sigma$) systems, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{conv:fig:coord-mismatch}.


Let the {\MPAS} global ocean mesh cover a region of interest with
$n_M$ Voronoi cells. Column $j$ contains $K_M$ geopotential $z^*$
layers with physical interfaces
$\{z^M_{j,k+1/2}\}_{k=0}^{K_M}$ and layer thicknesses
$\Delta z^M_{j,k}=|z^M_{j,k+1/2}-z^M_{j,k-1/2}|$. The level index is
common to all \MPAS columns, although the physical interfaces and wet
layer count can vary with free-surface elevation, bathymetry, and
masking. Let the \ROMS grid have $n_R$ horizontal cells. Column $i$
contains $K_R$ terrain-following ($\sigma$) layers with physical
interfaces $\{z^R_{i,\ell+1/2}\}_{\ell=0}^{K_R}$ and thicknesses
$\Delta z^R_{i,\ell}=|z^R_{i,\ell+1/2}-z^R_{i,\ell-1/2}|$, determined
by the local bathymetry $H_i$ and free-surface elevation $\zeta_i$
through the Song--Haidvogel stretching transformation
\citep{song1994semi,shchepetkin2005regional}. The discrete values are
finite-volume layer averages. An \MPAS field is
$\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_M\times K_M}$ and its projected \ROMS field is
$\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_R\times K_R}$. The downscaling problem is to
construct a consistent transfer $\Phi\mapsto\Psi$ while stating
separately which properties are provided by its horizontal and vertical
factors. The normalized horizontal remap preserves the area-weighted
integral over the shared horizontal domain, but it does not impose
conservation independently within each cell.

Note that a fully
three-dimensional intersection to compute a supermesh~\citep{farrell2009supermesh} of two such cell complexes can be computed, but in general will involve $O(n_M K_M n_R K_R)$ computational complexity to decipher candidate overlaps (worst case),
and can produce multiple sliver polyhedra whose quadratures may be quite ill-conditioned.
We will instead exploit the fact that the \MPAS{} $\zmpas$ coordinate shares a
common level indexing across all \MPAS columns, where level $k$ refers to
the same layer of the discretization everywhere in the global domain, even though its
physical thickness varies from column to column with the free surface, the bottom topography and vertical masking. A single horizontal map can therefore be
applied slab by slab at fixed $k$, which factors the volumetric transfer into a tensor product of a 
horizontal remap at each level followed with a one-dimensional vertical remap on each cell. The physical
depths that the vertical remap requires are recovered per column
afterwards, and so the factorization does not assume that the levels are
horizontally flat. Instead, we assume that interpolating within a
level index is meaningful, which holds when the physical $\zmpas$ surfaces vary slowly relative to the horizontal stencil.

Throughout the manuscript, the \MPAS model is referred to as the \emph{source} and \ROMS as the \emph{target}. However, note that the algorithmic design is agnostic to the structured or unstructured nature of the underlying topology. In terms of notation, we also use the subscript $j$ for \MPAS cell indices, and $i$ for \ROMS cell indices, while $k$ and $\ell$ index \MPAS and \ROMS vertical layers respectively. Uppercase $\Phi,\Xi,\Psi$ denote whole
three-dimensional arrays, with $\Xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n_R\times K_M}$
denoting the horizontally remapped field on the \MPAS level index. The
corresponding lowercase $\phi,\psi$ denote a single horizontal slab extracted at a fixed level, so that
$\phi_j=\Phi_{j,k}$ for a given level $k$. The cell areas are represented as $A^M_j$ for the \MPAS cell $j$, and $A^R_i$ for the \ROMS cell $i$, and $A_{ij}$
is the area of their intersection cell. All geometry is normalized to be on the unit
sphere, and spherical areas on the manifold are computed by \emph{L'Huilier}'s excess
formula on the unit sphere, though better options based on integrals over a spherical triangle using Gauss-quadrature \citep{ullrich2016tempestremap} are available as options.

Writing $\Xi$ as the intermediate field that results from transferring
every \MPAS level horizontally, the complete transfer is
\begin{equation}
  \Xi_{i,k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_M}W_{ij}\Phi_{j,k},\qquad
  \Psi_{i,\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_M}V^{(i)}_{\ell k}\Xi_{i,k},
  \label{conv:eq:tensor}
\end{equation}

where the horizontal map $W$ is reused unchanged at every \MPAS level
and the column-dependent vertical map $V^{(i)}$ acts independently on
each \ROMS column. This
prism-based 2D$\times$1D construction avoids sliver polyhedra while
retaining the column geometry that the vertical remap requires. The two
factors are in turn developed in \S \ref{sec:horizontal} and
\S\ref{sec:vertical}. A schematic of the volumetric remapping algorithm is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:remap_methods}.

\subsection{Horizontal remap as a sparse linear operator}\label{sec:horizontal}

Several choices are available for computing projections or interpolants from a global spherical manifold mesh like \MPAS onto an embedded \ROMS regional domain. The standard approach is the use of $L_2$ minimization~\citep{dukowicz1987accurate} that preserves both global and local integrals and is therefore exactly conservative. The low order variant of the minimizer is also truly property-preserving (monotone). %In the coarse-to-fine regime considered here it also imprints the \MPAS tessellation on the \ROMS field, which is why we do not use it for tracer transfer, as \S\ref{sec:why-not-fv} develops in detail and Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh-imprinting} demonstrates. We first set out both constructions.
Alternatively, one could also utilize variations of delaunay-based natural neighbor or bilinear interpolants, while preserving the integrals as designed in implementations like SCRIP~\citep{jones1999scrip}, ESMF~\citep{hill2004esmf} and TempestRemap~\citep{ullrich2015arbitrary}. The implications of some of these choices will be further explored.

For a fixed \MPAS depth level $k$, the horizontal factor $W$ of
Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:tensor} acts on the level slab
$\phi=\Phi_{:,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_M}$ as the sparse linear map
\begin{equation}
	\psi_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{n_M} W_{ij}\, \phi_j ,
	\qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{n_R \times n_M},
	\label{eq:linear-map}
\end{equation}
as defined by~\citet{ullrich2015arbitrary}. Two
specific constructions of the weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear-map} are
considered below, and they primarily differ in whether strict conservation or
smoothness is enforced for problem at hand.

\subsubsection{Conservative finite-volume (FV) map}
The first-order conservative finite-volume linear-map ~\citep{jones1999scrip} is computed based on the full supermesh in order to account for the contributions from all partial subcell geometry neighbors from the \MPAS mesh overlapping the \ROMS elements. This sets the
weight to the area of geometric overlap between \ROMS cell $i$ and
\MPAS cell $j$, normalized by the \ROMS area,
\begin{equation}
	W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij} \;=\; \frac{|\Omega_i \cap \Omega_j|}{|\Omega_i|}
	\;=\; \frac{A_{ij}}{A^R_i},
	\label{eq:fv-weight}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_i$, $\Omega_j$ are the (spherical) \ROMS and \MPAS
polygons and $A_{ij}$ their intersection area. This map is
\emph{conservative only over the \ROMS bounded subdomain}, a
condition that matters here because the \ROMS{} domain is a subset embedded in the global \MPAS{} mesh.

The \ROMS mesh is a bounded region embedded in the global \MPAS mesh, and so every \ROMS cell is labeled based on whether it is within the convex hull of the \MPAS coverage mesh~\citep{mahadevan2020coupling}. Let $f^M_j$ and $f^R_i$ denote the covered fractions of \MPAS cell $j$ and \ROMS cell $i$, obtained from the overlap areas $A_{ij}$ of the FV supermesh. Cells with $f=1$ are \emph{interior}, and those with $0<f<1$ are only \emph{partially} covered. The interior set, written as $\mathcal{I}$, is the support on which the error norms of \S\ref{conv:sec:verification-method} are evaluated.

Note that since
$\sum_i A_{ij} = A^M_j$ holds only for a \MPAS cell fully covered by the
union of \ROMS cells, the area-weighted integral identity
$\sum_i A^R_i \psi_i = \sum_j A^M_j \phi_j$ is exact for \MPAS cells
interior to the \ROMS boundary and needs to include a fractional weighting (computed from supermesh construction) for \MPAS cells that only partially overlaps with \ROMS domain. Hence, computing the conservation of fluxes and states in the \ROMS domain boundary strictly requires topological intersections, without which we
can only enforce weak conservation over $\mathcal{I}$. 

The conservative FV map is first-order accurate and, being
a convex average of the \MPAS values ($W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij}\ge 0$,
$\sum_j W^{\mathrm{FV}}_{ij}=1$ on fully-covered cells), it is
property-preserving on those cells i.e., the transferred values are bounded
by the \MPAS value range in the covering mesh.

\subsubsection{Normalized bilinear map}
The bilinear map locates each \ROMS cell centroid within the
\MPAS triangulation, forms bilinear interpolation weights
$b_{ij}$ from the enclosing \MPAS elements, and row-normalizes them, following the linear-map framework of~\citet{ullrich2015arbitrary,ullrich2016tempestremap}. The provisional map is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{W}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{ij}
  \;=\; \frac{b_{ij}}{\sum_{j'} b_{ij'}} ,
  \label{conv:eq:bilin-weight}
\end{equation}
so that constant fields are reproduced exactly
($\sum_j \widehat{W}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{ij}=1$). For a field slab $\phi$,
let $\widehat{\psi}_i=\sum_j\widehat{W}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{ij}\phi_j$.
The domain-wide normalization on the shared support is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=
 \frac{\sum_j A^M_j f^M_j\phi_j}
      {\sum_i A^R_i f^R_i\widehat{\psi}_i},
 \qquad
 \psi_i=\alpha\widehat{\psi}_i.
 \label{eq:bilin-global-normalization}
\end{equation}
This normalization preserves the area-weighted integral over the entire
shared domain. It is not locally conservative because the contribution
assigned to an individual \ROMS cell is not determined by overlap areas
as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight}. The provisional bilinear map is formally
second-order accurate on smooth fields \citep{ullrich2015arbitrary} and
does not tie the \ROMS values to the piecewise-constant average of one
\MPAS cell. When a \ROMS centroid lies within the enclosing \MPAS element,
the provisional weights are nonnegative and form a convex combination.
The domain-wide factor can alter this local bound when $\alpha\ne1$.
The second-order horizontal accuracy does not necessarily raise the order
of the composed tensor-product transfer, which is also limited by the
vertical remap of \S\ref{sec:vertical}, as discussed in
\S\ref{conv:sec:conv}.



\subsubsection{Issues with conservative FV map for tracers}\label{sec:why-not-fv}

The monotone, conservative finite-volume (FV) map in \eqref{eq:fv-weight} is the lowest-order case of the \(L_2\)-minimization framework used for general mesh remapping \citep{ullrich2015arbitrary,ullrich2016tempestremap}. Given compatible bases on the two meshes, the transferred field minimizes the error in the integral of the field defined on the overlapping domain between a source and target mesh topology
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega}(\phi-\psi)^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega ,
	\label{eq:l2-min}
\end{equation}
leading to a discrete system $\mathcal{W}\Phi=\Psi$, where $\mathcal{W}$ is the mass matrix over the \ROMS and \MPAS bases. Minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:l2-min} over the overlap domain is what makes the resulting map conservative, and at lowest order it recovers the area-ratio weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-weight}. Higher-order conservative, monotone, and smooth alternatives are available \citep{lauritzen2008monotone,lee1997mba}, but given the strong gradients in the 3D ocean states, we focus here only on the first-order FV map and the normalized bilinear map in \eqref{conv:eq:bilin-weight}, which capture the main trade-off relevant to \MPAS--\ROMS downscaling experiments.
%

This factor is not used for tracer soltion transfer in regional coupled runs however, to avoid mesh imprinting artifacts in the remapped fields.


%Under first-order FV remapping, all fine \ROMS cells lying within a given \MPAS Voronoi cell receive the same \MPAS cell average. This appears as a staircase pattern whose discontinuities follow \MPAS cell edges.

The conservative FV-based map projection result (center) reproduces the \MPAS Voronoi tessellation as piecewise-constant patches with sharp artificial steps, while the normalized bilinear projection (right) remains smooth.
%
These discontinuities are not physical gradients. They are introduced solely by the transfer operator and can inject grid-scale noise or adjustment features aligned with the \MPAS mesh when the field is used to initialize or force a regional model. The problem is especially consequential for fields that should vary smoothly at the regional scale. Bathymetry is the sharpest illustration because it sets the \ROMS sigma-coordinate geometry. An imprint in the transferred depth field perturbs the vertical grid itself, not only the horizontal representation, so the artifact propagates into every subsequent column operation.

The normalized bilinear remap removes the leading-order imprint by interpolating continuously across the \MPAS mesh rather than assigning constant cell averages. Equation~\eqref{eq:bilin-global-normalization} preserves the integral over the shared domain without enforcing conservation in each cell. This trade is appropriate for bathymetry, SSH, and initial-condition forcing because \ROMS evolves its tracer components with its own conservative transport scheme, whereas mesh-imprinted forcing introduces artificial gradients that can persist in the interior solution. The domain-wide correction can modify local bounds in regions with strong gradients~\citep{mahadevan2022mira}.

Higher-order quasi-interpolants offer a third route. Multilevel B-spline approximation (MBA) builds a smooth field from a hierarchy of control lattices~\citep{lee1997mba}, while Generalized Moving Least-Squares and Weighted Essentially Non-Oscillatory reconstructions instead fit local stencils, or nonlinear weights that can suppress oscillations near discontinuities~\citep{mahadevan2022mira}. Each of these different quasi-interpolants in general would need a normalization step to recover conservation, and limiters to recover monotonicity. We have not pursued these extensions in the current study.

\subsection{Vertical remap of each column}\label{sec:vertical}

For each \ROMS column $i$, let $j(i)$ denote the \MPAS column selected
by the nearest-column geometry rule. Its physical interfaces define the
\MPAS intervals
\begin{equation}
 I^M_{i,k}=[z^M_{j(i),k-1/2},z^M_{j(i),k+1/2}],
 \qquad
 I^R_{i,\ell}=[z^R_{i,\ell-1/2},z^R_{i,\ell+1/2}],
 \label{eq:vertical-intervals}
\end{equation}
where interval lengths are independent of the sign convention for depth.
Assigning one \MPAS column geometry to $\Xi_{i,:}$ is an approximation.
The values in that intermediate column combine the horizontal stencil
through $W$, whereas the physical layer interfaces come from $j(i)$. The
\ROMS interfaces follow from the local \ROMS bathymetry and free-surface
elevation.

The production vertical operator treats $\Xi_{i,k}$ as the average of a
piecewise-constant reconstruction over $I^M_{i,k}$. Define the overlap
length and \ROMS layer thickness by
\begin{equation}
 d_{i,\ell k}=|I^R_{i,\ell}\cap I^M_{i,k}|,
 \qquad \Delta z^R_{i,\ell}=|I^R_{i,\ell}|.
 \label{eq:vertical-overlap}
\end{equation}
On common vertical support, the entries of the column map in
Eq.~\eqref{conv:eq:tensor} are
\begin{equation}
 V^{(i)}_{\ell k}=\frac{d_{i,\ell k}}{\Delta z^R_{i,\ell}},
 \qquad
 \Psi_{i,\ell}=\frac{1}{\Delta z^R_{i,\ell}}
   \sum_{k=1}^{K_M}d_{i,\ell k}\Xi_{i,k}.
 \label{eq:vertical-map}
\end{equation}
Thus each \ROMS layer integral is constructed from the overlapping
\MPAS layer contributions,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta z^R_{i,\ell}\Psi_{i,\ell}
 =\sum_{k=1}^{K_M}d_{i,\ell k}\Xi_{i,k}.
 \label{eq:vertical-conservation}
\end{equation}
Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:vertical-conservation} over $\ell$ preserves the
column integral of the piecewise-constant \MPAS representation when the
\MPAS and \ROMS intervals cover the same physical space. The horizontal
normalization separately preserves the area-weighted integral over the
shared horizontal domain. It does not, however, impose conservation within each
cell locally. These properties do not by themselves establish
conservation of the volume-integral in the complete three-dimensional transfer
when physical layer thickness varies horizontally. The construction is the
first-order finite-volume form
of conservative remapping between generalized vertical coordinates
\citep{white2009highorder}.

A monotone, piecewise cubic-Hermite interpolant (PCHIP) is evaluated in
\S\ref{conv:sec:axial-refinement} as a separate quasi-interpolant method. It
samples an analytic profile at \MPAS interfaces and evaluates the
interpolant at \ROMS interfaces. It neither consumes nor produces the
layer-average degrees of freedom in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vertical-map}. It can
converge faster for smooth profiles but does not satisfy the layer-integral
identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vertical-conservation}, which is why the
conservative layer-average map is used for the three-dimensional transfers
reported here.

We want to emphasize that while most regions of the embedded nesting is consistent, two situations require the column to be extended beyond the range \MPAS actually resolves. Supplying a full level slab requires values below the deepest wet \MPAS layer, where \MPAS masks its vertical layers. These values are held at the deepest wet value, equivalent to nearest-neighbor continuation.%, and the artificial tail is discarded when the column is restricted to its physical depth range. 
Where a \ROMS column extends beyond the physical \MPAS column height, tracer values are likewise held at the nearest endpoint, while velocities are set to zero to impose a no-flow condition. Integrals over these extended \ROMS regions include the prescribed continuation and are therefore not part of the conservation identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vertical-conservation}. These choices matter more specifically where bathymetry varies sharply, and we return to their consequences in \S\ref{conv:sec:bathysmooth}.


%We should emphasize that these are properties of the two component operators. Neither individually, nor in combination, do they guarantee that the composed three-dimensional transfer conserves volume-integrated quantities or preserves bounds.

It is noted that the zonal and meridional velocities are transferred component-wise as scalar, Earth-relative fields at horizontal cell centers. No rotations to the curvilinear basis, interpolations to polygonal edges, or discrete divergence constraints are imposed during this transfer. The velocity diagnostics presented here measure componentwise transport in a fixed basis. Alternatively, the use of consistent mimetic schemes to dynamically transfer vector fields from \MPAS cell edges directly to \ROMS cell edges in a conservative manner remains outside the present scope of the paper and will be pursued in the future to directly replace the three-level remap worfflow: \MPAS cell edge to center, \MPAS cell center to \ROMS cell center and \ROMS cell center to cell edges.
