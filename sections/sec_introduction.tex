Global ocean models are integrated over decadal time scales, which constrains the horizontal and temporal resolution that can be used in global simulations. As a result, many dynamically important processes are only partly resolved or remain unresolved, including mesoscale eddies with scales of approximately 10--100 km, mixed-layer instabilities, and submesoscale fronts. These processes influence heat transport, circulation, restratification, and tracer exchange \citep{boccaletti2007mixed,foxkemper2008mle,thomas2008submesoscale,taylor2023submesoscale}. Their effects must therefore be represented through parameterizations whose behavior strongly depends on model resolutions \citep{hallberg2013using}, the consequences of which are particularly apparent near coastal fronts, river plumes, shelf breaks, and western boundary currents, where circulation and tracer pathways can remain sensitive to grid spacing \citep{chassignet2017impact}.

One-way global-to-regional coupling increases resolution over a selected domain without imposing the same resolution throughout the global ocean. In the configuration considered here, the ocean component of the Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM)~\citep{golaz2019e3sm}, \MPAS-Ocean~\citep{ringler2013multi,hoch2020MPAS}, supplies the evolving large-scale state, including initial and lateral boundary fields, to refined domains simulated with the Regional Ocean Modeling System (\ROMS)~\citep{shchepetkin2005regional}. ROMS is widely used for structured, boundary-fitted regional applications and its terrain-following coordinate permits detailed representation of coastal and open-ocean bathymetry. Regional grids can therefore represent coastline geometry, bathymetry, and local forcing that are not resolved by the global \MPAS mesh. Refinement does not reconstruct information absent from the global solution. The transferred state supplies the large-scale environment from which finer-scale variability can develop under the regional dynamics and forcing.

Ocean nesting in this context has a well established progression going from one-way embedding to online and two-way coupling between global and regional models. \citet{penven2006embedding} evaluated one-way \ROMS embedding in which a coarse global model supplied initial and lateral boundary conditions. \citet{mason2010nesting} developed offline nesting procedures that combine interpolation with model-aware preparation of regional forcing and volume-transport correction. Open-boundary studies further show that accurate interpolation is not sufficient for a stable long integration. Incoming information, outgoing radiation, relaxation, and net transport must remain dynamically compatible with the regional model \citep{marchesiello2001open,blayo2005boundary,oddo2008boundary}. Online and two-way methods add synchronization and feedback from the regional model to the global model, making conservation and compatibility across the nest interface more restrictive \citep{debreu2008embedding,debreu2012nesting}. The present work remains offline and one-way, but it addresses the field-transfer problem that must be resolved before such exchanges can be used reliably between ocean models resolving multiple global and regional scales.

The consistent field transfer of both tracer (salinity, temperature) and velocity fields is uniquely complicated for the \MPAS--\ROMS model combination. The \MPAS ocean model represents the global state on a variable-resolution unstructured polygonal mesh with geopotential $z^*$ layers, whereas \ROMS uses a structured curvilinear horizontal grid and terrain-following vertical coordinates. Their wet masks, bathymetry, horizontal support, and physical layer interfaces need not coincide. Conservative spherical remapping and supermesh methods preserve defined integrals across nonmatching horizontal grids \citep{jones1999scrip,farrell2009supermesh}, while general linear remap frameworks distinguish conservation from consistency, accuracy, and monotonicity on arbitrary meshes \citep{ullrich2015arbitrary,lauritzen2008monotone}. Ocean vertical-coordinate remapping presents a separate reconstruction and conservation problem \citep{white2009highorder}. This implies that constant-preserving horizontal interpolation, integral conservation, boundedness, and stable boundary forcing are distinct requirements that must in general be balanced. Note that satisfying one constraint does not necessarily establish the others, and composing horizontal and vertical operators requires an explicit representation of both the global and regional model's three-dimensional column geometry. These constraints impose significant constraints on the infrastructure that can handle both one-way and two-way coupling between these models.

General frameworks such as ESMF and MCT provide component interfaces, grid descriptions, regridding services, and parallel communication for coupled models \citep{hill2004esmf,larson2005mct}. Here, we use the Mesh Oriented datABase (\MOAB) \citep{moab521_2020} which supplies the complete mesh representation, overlap geometry, fields defined as tags on mesh entities, and the linear sparse-operator infrastructure already being used by the E3SM coupler \citep{mahadevan2020coupling}. Based on this infrastructure, we formulate and evaluate a separable volumetric transfer from an unstructured global ocean mesh to structured curvilinear regional grids with different vertical coordinates. A horizontal sparse map is reused over the \MPAS level slabs and followed by an independent conservative layer-average remap in each \ROMS column, avoiding construction of a full three-dimensional polyhedral intersection. %For the reported downscaling experiments, normalized bilinear horizontal interpolation reduces coarse-source mesh imprinting, while finite-volume overlap geometry supplies coverage and conservative references.

In order to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed approach to couple global to regional models, we design experiments using four \ROMS regional domains that introduce land masks, steep bathymetric gradients, and estuarine geometry. Their nominal horizontal resolutions range from approximately \SI{4}{\kilo\metre} to \SI{0.6}{\kilo\metre}. The verification studies use characteristic lengths based on the shared overlap area, defined in \S\ref{conv:sec:verification-method}.

The first is a North Atlantic implementation of the Near Inertial Shear and Kinetic Energy in the North Atlantic Experiment (NISKINe) model \citep{simmons2024niskine}, designed to study the generation, propagation, and dissipation of near-inertial waves and their interaction with mesoscale and submesoscale structure in the Iceland Basin. Its nominal resolution is \SI{2}{\kilo\metre} over water approximately \SI{2750}{\metre} deep and carries no land mask, so it exercises the transfer with neither masking nor sharp topography.

The Gulf Stream domain adds bathymetric relief. Its curvilinear mesh, with a characteristic length of \SI{1.6}{\kilo\metre}, follows the meandering path of the western boundary current, and it also carries no land mask, but including the southern edge of Georges Bank gives it substantially sharper bathymetric gradients than the open-ocean case.
%
The third domain covers most of the Bay of Bengal with a shared-area characteristic length of \SI{3.70}{\kilo\metre}. It adds a nearshore setting with land masks on all sides, steep bathymetric gradients, and river inputs drawn from the GloFAS-ERA5 global river discharge reanalysis \citep{harrigan2020glofas}. The fourth is the Chesapeake Bay application developed to study the sensitivity of estuarine hypoxia to external physical forcing \citep{st2024sensitivity}. Its estuarine scales fall well below what the local \MPAS grid represents and much of the domain is masked in \MPAS, so it places the greatest demand on the transfer of the four. Their geographic extent and bathymetric characteristics are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:zed_comparison}.
Together, these domains allow us to assess the reliability and limitations of remapped global states as initial and boundary conditions for regional simulations, and to establish a foundation for subsequent studies of overturning circulation, western boundary-current energetics, and exchanges between the open ocean and river-influenced coastal environments.
